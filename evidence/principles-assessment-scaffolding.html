Principles for Good MLL Assessment Scaffolding

CIA First International School — MLL Support

Developed AY 2025-26

What is Tier 3 scaffolding?

Tier 3 scaffolding is targeted support for students with the highest language needs. In the context of assessment, it means giving MLL students the language and organisational tools they need to show what they know — without lowering what we expect them to know.

The same task. The same rubric. Reduced language and cognitive barriers.

Why we scaffold assessments this way

MLL students are not less capable. They are demonstrating understanding through a second, third, or fourth language. A student who cannot construct an academic English sentence about theme does not necessarily lack understanding of theme — they lack the language to express it. Our job is to bridge that gap, not to accept a lower standard of evidence.

At the same time, over-scaffolding creates its own problems:

  • A handout that looks more complicated than the mainstream one increases anxiety
  • Too many steps signals to students that their version is harder, not easier
  • Scaffolds that do the thinking for the student produce responses that don't reflect actual understanding

Good scaffolding is invisible support — it feels like a helping hand, not a different task.

The process: how we build a scaffolded assessment

Scaffolding an assessment well requires more than reformatting the mainstream handout. It requires understanding what was taught and making deliberate decisions together. The process has three stages:

Stage 1: Understand what was taught

Before looking at the assessment, look at the unit. This means reading the unit overview, the cornerstone lessons, and understanding which standards were prioritised and what texts and activities students encountered. MLL teachers cannot scaffold an assessment fairly if they do not know what students have been exposed to.

Key questions:

  • What are the priority standards for this unit?
  • What vocabulary and concepts have MLL students encountered in class?
  • Did all teachers cover the content in the same way?

Stage 2: Analyse the mainstream assessment

Read the task sheet, student handout, and rubric. Identify:

  • What each task is actually asking (separate the cognitive demand from the language demand)
  • What scaffolding already exists in the mainstream version
  • What the rubric rewards — the scaffold must target the same criteria
  • Where the biggest language barriers are

Stage 3: Make deliberate scaffold decisions

Before building anything, decide:

  • Which texts or materials to use (full text or excerpt? teacher-selected or student choice?)
  • Which parts of the task need the most support
  • What kind of scaffold fits each part (sentence frame, word bank, organiser, example, drawing space)
  • What the handout should look like so it doesn't appear harder than the mainstream

Design principles

1. Match the visual weight of the mainstream version

If the mainstream handout has one organiser and a list of sentence frames, the MLL version should also look like one organiser and a list of sentence frames. More visible sections = more perceived difficulty, even if each section is simpler.

2. Embed sentence frames where students write

Sentence frames listed on a separate page or at the top of a task require students to look back and forth. Put them directly on the page, inside the cell, next to the writing lines — right where they are needed.

3. Use word banks with examples, not prescriptions

Word banks should suggest useful vocabulary without locking students into it. Use category headings (Feelings / Actions / Describing words) and example words from the most common text. Add a note that students can use words from their own text.

4. Every prompt needs an example

Every sentence frame, every writing prompt, every fill-in-the-blank should have a small example in italics underneath it. Examples reduce paralysis, especially for students who are unsure whether they are on the right track.

5. Break analytical prompts into two steps

When a task asks students to analyse or explain (e.g. "explain why this is important"), break it into two frames:

This is important because it shows that ___.
Without this, the story would ___.

This reduces the cognitive demand without reducing the expectation. The student still has to reason — they just have a structure to put that reasoning into.

6. Keep drawing where the mainstream keeps drawing

If the mainstream task allows students to draw, keep it. Drawing reduces writing anxiety and still demonstrates comprehension. It is not a concession — it is a legitimate response mode.

7. Align the final task to the rubric

Do not include tasks or prompts that are not assessed. Open-ended "favourite part" prompts, while engaging, do not appear on the rubric. Replace them with something that does — for example, a theme connection frame.

8. Use colour consistently and purposefully

Colour helps students navigate the handout. Use one consistent accent colour for section headers. Use distinct colours when comparing two things (e.g., purple for one text, orange for another). Colour is a scaffold in itself.

Output format

All scaffolded assessment handouts should be produced as .docx files.

Why:

  • .docx files can be uploaded directly to Google Drive and opened as Google Docs for editing and sharing
  • They can be edited on Windows computers by any teacher
  • They can be printed from Google Docs or opened in Microsoft Word
  • They preserve formatting better than plain text when shared across devices

When formatting:

  • Use tables for all structured sections (organisers, word banks, comparison charts) — tables survive conversion better than manual spacing
  • Use filled colour bars for section headers — these convert cleanly
  • Always include the school name, year, and assessment name in a header bar at the top of each task page
  • Include the rubric in the same document so teachers and students have everything in one place

What makes a scaffolded assessment good?

Ask these questions before sharing any scaffolded handout:

QuestionWhy it matters
Does it look simpler than the mainstream version at a glance?Visual complexity signals difficulty before a student reads a word
Is every prompt tied to something the rubric rewards?We are scaffolding for assessment success, not for engagement
Are the instructions unambiguous for a student with limited English?Instructions are often the first barrier
Could an MLL teacher hand this to a student with minimal extra explanation?The scaffold should be self-sufficient
Does the word bank contain words actually useful for this specific task?Generic word banks are low value
Does the handout avoid doing the thinking for the student?Scaffolds that pre-fill answers produce meaningless data

What scaffolding is NOT

  • It is not a simpler version of the task
  • It is not a reduction in expected standard
  • It is not a translation
  • It is not a summary of the text provided for students
  • It is not something that can be built from the mainstream handout alone, without understanding the unit

A note on sharing this work

Scaffolded assessments take significant time to build well. When a scaffold is built for one MLL group, it should be:

  • Saved to the MLL shared drive in the relevant subject folder
  • Shared with all MLL teachers working with students on that assessment
  • Archived for reference in future years when similar units or assessment types arise

The principles above apply across subjects and year levels. While the specific scaffolds will vary — a maths quiz looks different from a reading literature task — the underlying methodology is the same.
