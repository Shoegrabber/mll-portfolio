Every task in a classroom makes two demands: what you have to think, and what you have to say. I separate them, and rebuild the second one.

In practice that means making assessments answerable by students who don't yet have the English to answer them — without changing what is being assessed. Same task, same rubric, fewer barriers between what a student knows and what they can show. The same analysis applies to instruction, to numeracy, and to adult language learners. I wrote the method down, and I build the tools that make it repeatable.

01 — Data practice

From diagnostic to evidence

Individual Learning Plans
Caseload of 16, groups of five
Built 2025–26
The demand

Each student's ILP lived in their own shared Drive folder. Writing session notes for a group of five meant opening five folder inventories, finding five files, and typing five separate reflections — while trying to remember what each student's goals actually were. For a ninety-minute intervention, the documentation cost more than the planning did. The predictable result across any team working this way is that notes get written late, written generically, or not written at all.

What I did

Built a single Google Form as the entry point. Pick the group; it lists the students. One field for the shared task, which copies into every student's note. One field per student for the individual observation — with that student's current goals displayed right there beside it, the same goals printed in their goal booklet. Submit once, and five ILP files are updated with specific, individual notes.

It runs on a phone, so notes get captured during the session with voice transcription and finished at a computer afterwards. I never open the ILP file.

Before — per session, per group of five
  1. Open five shared Drive folders
  2. Locate five ILP documents
  3. Look up each student's goals separately
  4. Retype the shared task five times
  5. Write five individual notes, from memory, after the fact
After — same session
  1. Select the group
  2. Type the shared task once
  3. Write five individual notes against the goals shown
  4. Submit — five files updated
  5. Capture in-session on a phone; finish later
What it shows

I automated the mechanism and none of the content — every observation and professional judgement is written by me. The gain wasn't speed for its own sake. Because the form put each student's goals in front of me at the moment of writing, every note got attached to a goal instead of floating free. The tool made the right practice the easy path, and the documentation became more specific as a consequence of becoming faster.

02 — Assessment design

Silk Road Narrative, Tier 1 / 2 / 3

Grade 4 performance task
Social Studies + English
Three scaffold levels, one rubric
The demand

A narrative performance task assessed across two subjects at once, for a cohort spanning WIDA Entering to Bridging. The standard could not move. The rubric could not move.

What I did

Built three versions of the booklet. Put them side by side and the claim checks itself — the task prompt is word for word identical in all three.

Identical in Tier 1, 2 and 3
“You are a storyteller. Tell the story of a merchant's journey along the Silk Road — the people, the problems, the choices, and what trade changed.”
Tier 1 — mainstream
  • Plans independently from a dense block of location prose — Samarkand, the Pamir passes at 4,600m, Baghdad in the Golden Age
  • Process is implicit; the student decides how to work
  • Writes from a blank page
Tier 2
  • Same four parts, each given an explicit job — the Beginning is who you are and why you are going
  • Work chunked into three named sessions
  • Each session runs READ → FIND → WRITE against a mentor paragraph
Tier 3
  • Same structure as Tier 2, with the Beginning reduced to who you are and where you are going
  • Mentor paragraph supplied for every part
  • Writing happens in completion frames, not open prose
Same page of the same task in each booklet. What changes across the tiers is that the writing process becomes visible and the language gets supplied. What never changes is the task, the four-part narrative structure, or what the rubric rewards.

03 — Framework

Principles for Good MLL Assessment Scaffolding

Written 2026
My own working method
Shared with the MLL team
The demand

I needed a consistent answer to the question that actually matters, and I needed it to be the same answer every time: at what point does support stop being support and start doing the thinking for the student? Working it out case by case produces scaffolds that drift.

What I did

Wrote the method down for myself. Three stages — understand what was taught, analyse the mainstream assessment, then make deliberate scaffold decisions before building anything. Eight design principles and a quality checklist underneath. I then used the document as the specification for AI-assisted scaffold production, so the same standard applied whether I built a booklet by hand or with a tool. Writing my method down precisely enough that a machine could follow it is what made it repeatable.

What it shows

The principles are counterintuitive in a way that only comes from doing the work. One of them is about visual weight: if the mainstream handout has one organiser and a list of frames, the scaffolded version should also look like one organiser and a list of frames. More visible sections read as harder to a student, even when every section is simpler. Good scaffolding is invisible support — a helping hand, not a different task.

That principle is a graphic design judgement applied to assessment equity, and I notice it because I trained as a designer before I trained as a teacher.

04 — Tool I built

SFL Mentor Text Guide

Live · four lessons
React + TypeScript
Proof of concept
Open the tool →
The demand

A sentence frame on paper is static. The student can't hear the sentence, can't see how a paragraph is assembled from language functions, and can't move the parts around to find out what happens.

What I did

Grade 4 Social Studies — Community Adaptations (Cause & Effect). Sentence bank left, mentor paragraph right, colour by language function.

Built it. A browser tool where students drag sentences into a paragraph, colour-coded by Systemic Functional Linguistics function — Sequence, Cause & Effect, How It Works, Contrast, Conclusion — with read-aloud and three levels of granularity: sentence, phrase, discourse.

What it shows

Four lessons across Science, Social Studies and Argument, written with culturally relevant content — the Community Adaptations lessons are built on Cambodian river communities, not generic textbook examples. It runs in a browser, on a school network, with no login. Working software, not a claim about technology.

A proof of concept — I built and shipped it, but it was never put in front of a class. I am showing it as evidence of what I can build, not as a classroom result.
